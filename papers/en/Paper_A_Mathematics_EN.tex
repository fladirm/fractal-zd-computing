\section{Fractal Zero-Divisor Computing --- A: Signed Cayley--Dickson
Information Forest and Higher-π
Invariants}\label{fractal-zero-divisor-computing-a-signed-cayleydickson-information-forest-and-higher-ux3c0-invariants}

\textbf{Author:} Vladimír Bíža\\
\textbf{Research manuscript · English technical edition · 25 October
2026}\\
\textbf{Scope:} Mathematics and computer science; independent of any
physical theory.

\subsection{Abstract}\label{abstract}

We develop a recursively addressable, signed multi-channel atlas of
two-term zero-divisor relations in the real Cayley--Dickson tower. An
exact XOR-and-plaquette predicate classifies selected annihilating
pairs; inherited families exhibit fourfold lifts, mutually annihilating
subspaces and tensor-factorized adjacency, while new relations are born
at every doubling. A separate algebraic phase refinement defines a
source period converging to π and supplies closed-form volume and
norm-defect invariants. We specify the exact domain of each count,
distinguish pairwise edges from higher-order information, and provide
reference validation contracts.

\subsection{1. Canonical Cayley--Dickson source
algebra}\label{canonical-cayleydickson-source-algebra}

We use the real positive-definite, non-split Cayley--Dickson tower with
the multiplication convention displayed below. The basis multiplication
is a signed XOR permutation. The sign is not a lookup-table primitive:
its recurrence follows from doubling and can be evaluated using
logarithmically many stages. The convention is part of the
reproducibility contract; switching conventions without an explicit
isomorphism invalidates comparison of signed witnesses.

The tower becomes nonassociative at the octonions and, beginning with
the sedenions, loses alternativity and develops nontrivial zero
divisors. Here a zero divisor is a nonzero element having zero product
with another nonzero element, not a floating-point underflow.

(a,b)(c,d)=(ac-\bar d b,\;da+b\bar c).
\tag{1}
\]

\[
e_a e_b=\varepsilon_k(a,b)e_{a\oplus b},\qquad \varepsilon_k(a,b)\in\{-1,1\}.
\tag{2}
\]

\subsection{2. Four-address zero-product
predicate}\label{four-address-zero-product-predicate}

An assessor is an unordered pair of distinct nonzero basis addresses,
together with a choice of sign on the corresponding two-term imaginary
vector. Two disjoint assessors can cancel their product only if their
XOR colors coincide. Expanding the signed basis rule produces two
coefficients on different XOR addresses; both must vanish. Their
compatibility is the four-sign plaquette invariant W.

\textbf{Theorem 1 (complete two-term predicate).} For disjoint assessors
with equal XOR color, there exist nonzero signed directions whose
product vanishes if and only if W=+1. The relative signs must satisfy
the displayed condition. \textbf{Proof.} Equate the two coefficients of
the product to zero, multiply the conditions to obtain W=+1, and
back-substitute to select the relative sign. In every implementation the
shared-color and disjointness checks precede the plaquette test.

\[
xy=(\varepsilon_{ac}+\sigma\tau\varepsilon_{bd})e_{a\oplus c}+(\tau\varepsilon_{ad}+\sigma\varepsilon_{bc})e_{a\oplus d}.
\tag{4}
\]

\[
W(a,b;c,d)=\varepsilon_{ac}\varepsilon_{bd}\varepsilon_{ad}\varepsilon_{bc}.
\tag{5}
\]

\subsection{3. The K16 root, its spectrum, and the nonzero
complement}\label{the-k16-root-its-spectrum-and-the-nonzero-complement}

At K16 the seven active colors are 9 through 15. Each has six unsigned

zero-product assessor edges. There are 315 candidate assessor pairs and

concern two-term assessors, not all zero divisors in the continuous
algebraic variety.

\subsection{4. Fourfold inheritance and mutually annihilating
subspaces}\label{fourfold-inheritance-and-mutually-annihilating-subspaces}

\textbf{Theorem 2 (four-lift).} Let x and y be orthogonal, purely
imaginary elements of K\_N with xy=yx=0. Doubling produces four mixed
descendants whose products vanish in either order. \textbf{Proof.}
Substitute all four combinations of (x,0), (0,x), (y,0), and (0,y) into
the Cayley--Dickson multiplication law. Each component is an old zero
product or its conjugate. Induct on the number of doublings.

This does more than clone edges. By bilinearity, the descendants span
two independent subspaces each of dimension 2\^{}t, and every
cross-product between those subspaces vanishes. Passing to nonzero
directions modulo real scaling yields a projective product variety. For
t=7 the two 128-dimensional spaces have 16,384 basis-pair combinations;
both product orientations yield 32,768 vanishing products.

\[
x_0=(x,0),\quad x_1=(0,x),\quad y_0=(y,0),\quad y_1=(0,y).

\[

\dim V_x^{(t)}=\dim V_y^{(t)}=2^t.
\tag{8}
\]

\[
\mathbb{RP}^{2^t-1}\times\mathbb{RP}^{2^t-1}\subseteq\mathscr Z_{k+t}^{\mathrm{proj}}.

\subsection{5. Factorized spectra and the genealogical
boundary}\label{factorized-spectra-and-the-genealogical-boundary}

\textbf{Theorem 3 (inherited-component factorization).} For an unchanged
root color, each edge lifts to a complete bipartite four-edge block
while nonedges stay nonedges. Consequently its adjacency is the
Kronecker product of the root adjacency and an all-ones matrix. This
statement applies to the inherited component, not to every newly born
edge in the full tower.

The spectrum follows from the spectrum of K(2,2,2) and the all-ones
factor: one positive and two negative nonzero eigenvalues, scaled by
2\^{}t, with a growing zero eigenspace. The normalized nonzero spectrum
remains fixed. The sequence of ancestry words has a Cantor-type boundary
whose tripartite graphon is the limit of this particular inherited
component.

\subsection{7. Multiple algebraic information
channels}\label{multiple-algebraic-information-channels}

We preserve at least seven distinguishable channels: zero product (ZD),
nonzero product (NZD), shared incidence, commutator, associator,
alternator, and birth. A zero pairwise product does not imply that all
higher products involving a third input also vanish.

For a concrete sedenion witness, take x=e\_2+e\_11, y=e\_4−e\_13, and
z=e\_1. The direct product xy is zero but the associator (xz)y−x(zy) is
−4e\_7. An index that records only the pairwise zero-product graph
irrevocably drops that higher-order distinction. Observability may also
be analyzed by Krylov ranks over selected operator probes;
application-level semantics must be supplied independently of the
algebra.

\subsection{8. Higher-π: a source period before geometric
volumes}\label{higher-ux3c0-a-source-period-before-geometric-volumes}

The first associative phase plane is the K2 subalgebra generated by a
unit imaginary Y. A real scalar character of order three is impossible
in K1 but an algebraic cubic root of unity exists in K2. The integer
lattice quotient Λ24/3Λ24 provides a natural finite group on which such
a character acts; it is an algebraic construction, not a claim about
physical spacetime.

and ternary refinements be defined as shown in the equations. Their
inscribed and tangent half-perimeters form algebraic lower and upper
approximants whose difference decays quadratically with cycle length.
Both converge to the classical constant π. \textbf{Proof.} Use
u\_\{r+1\}³=u\_r, identify \textbar1−u\_r\textbar{} as the corresponding
polygon chord, establish the familiar polygon bounds, and take the
common limit. The numerical value π is not supplied to the recursion.
The source-defined limit is identified geometrically only after its
algebraic construction.

same period. Every unit imaginary vector of K\_N generates a complex

changes with N is the dimension of the family of planes and the

phase-plane formulas, and their ratios yield the central binomial fusion
coefficients.

\subsection{10. Algebraic synthesis}\label{algebraic-synthesis}

The construction is a signed, typed and locally reconstructible
information forest rather than a static graph or a list of special
constants. It combines an exact address oracle, recursively inherited
annihilating subspaces, a separate newborn-edge budget, analyzable
spectral limits, and exact moments of norm nonmultiplicativity. Its
usefulness for computing rests on preserving all these layers
separately.

\subsection{11. Object boundaries and inverse
addressing}\label{object-boundaries-and-inverse-addressing}

We distinguish four carriers: basis addresses; signed two-term
assessors; their selected unsigned zero-product graph; and the
continuous variety of all zero divisors. Numerical edge counts describe
only the third. Projective subspaces furnish explicit subsets of the
fourth. Conflating them would produce wildly misleading scalability
figures.

The canonical signed key stores level, XOR color, the two sorted
assessor pairs, relative signs, orientation and ancestry. Lexicographic
canonicalization deduplicates a chosen root family but need not
establish global identity across different roots or equivalent geometric
directions. A product of at least three factors must carry its full
parenthesization.

two t-bit words uniquely determine inherited descendants using the two

\textbf{Proof.} Induct on ancestry length and apply the four-lift at
each step.

\subsection{13. Reproducibility
contract}\label{reproducibility-contract}

A conforming reference implementation verifies signed XOR
multiplication, unit-square signs, the explicit K16 zero-product
witness, the K16 84/315 counts, K32 and K64 census, equality of explicit
and Kronecker-inherited operator application, and the exact rational
invariants. Signed products of general sparse vectors require bilinear
accumulation. Each system should run its own algebraic tests before it
reports throughput.

\subsection{14. Final mathematical
statement}\label{final-mathematical-statement}

At every level k≥4 of the standard nonsplit real Cayley--Dickson tower,
a uniform signed-address oracle exists. Its designated two-term assessor
atlas has a fully specified predicate, inherited annihilator families,
and a separate census of genuinely new edges. The same atlas determines
rational spherical norm moments and their higher-π compounds. The
infinite structure is described as a compatible sequence of finite
computations, not as an infinite matrix stored in memory.
